# TWT Website — Project Context

## What this is

Thermal Wall Technologies, LLC (founder: Michael Sandefur) has a patented building wall/floor system: concrete with embedded hydronic tubing, insulated on the exterior only, so the concrete's thermal mass becomes a low-temperature radiator and heat store. It runs at ~65–75°F instead of the ~105–120°F conventional hydronic/heat pump systems need — that lower operating temperature is the one mechanical fact behind every efficiency claim (heat pump gain, solar collection gain, passive cooling via thermal mass).

Build the newer patented block-based version (dry-stackable insulated concrete block system, US Patent 11,142,908 / reissue USRE50072E1, active, also granted in Canada and the UK, through 2039). The earlier single-sided ICF-with-plywood version from older marketing material is not the current product — mention only as brief development history if at all.

Current stage: nothing is field-measured yet. Everything published is engineering calculation, independently cross-checked by University of Southern Indiana engineering faculty (Dr. Brandon Field), whose model came within ~2% of the founder's own numbers. This is a real, meaningful validation — not a measured result from a real building, but not a weak one either: the founder has spent close to a decade building and refining this modeling himself, across dozens of scenarios, and getting an independent PhD-level engineering cross-check that close is a genuine achievement, not a footnote.

## What the site is for

This is not a finished-product marketing site. The company needs serious funded validation — real testing in a real structure — before any commercial rollout. The site's job is to attract that validation and partnership, while giving the general public an honest, confident picture of what this is and what it could become.

Three balanced audiences, each with its own destination page after a shared homepage:

1. Academic & research institutions — propose a research collaboration
2. Corporate & technology partners — co-development, licensing exploration, integration
3. Homeowners & general public — honest, informative, explicitly framed as future, not for sale today

Remember who is actually reading this. Academic leadership, corporate partners, and builders evaluate opportunities commercially before they evaluate them technically — a site that reads as unsure of itself, or that leads with everything unproven before establishing what's real, reads as a team that isn't serious about commercializing its own work. That is the opposite of the goal. See **Voice: confident, not defensive** below — it is as load-bearing as any content rule in this document.

The homepage tells one shared story to all three (mechanism → evidence → what needs validating) because the same rigor builds trust with all of them. They diverge only after that, into three roughly equal-weight pages.

## Homepage — section-by-section spec

Build in this order. Every section needs a plain-English eyebrow label (not just a color) so category is never color-only — this is an accessibility requirement, not a style choice.

1. **Hero** — plainly states what TWT is and what it does, leads with the mechanism and the achievement (patented, independently cross-checked), states that it's seeking research/validation partners as the next step, not as the headline. No hype, no unattributed numbers, no apology.
2. **Core operating principle** — the 65–75°F vs. ~105–120°F comparison, framed as the company's engineering result, independently checked — not as a hedge-first "hypothesis." Name the specific conventional system being compared, not "conventional systems" generically.
3. **How TWT works** — assembly diagram + plain explanation, plus a short distinct block answering "isn't this just a normal ICF?" — with the actual mechanical difference (block geometry, insulation/facade attachment), not just a reassurance that it's different.
4. **Evidence ladder** — ordered from most- to least-certain: established building science → founder's own calculations → independent university check → established ICF precedent → performance claims not yet field-measured. Patents do not belong in this section — a patent proves the idea is original, not that it works. State each rung's evidence label once, clearly. Do not restate "this isn't proof" more than once per page — say it once, well, and trust the reader.
5. **What must be validated** — reads as an actual research plan: open questions, required testing/instrumentation, needed expertise, milestones, success criteria. This is the real "ask" of the page — give it real substance, drawn from the specific engineering constraints already identified (not generic placeholders). Ends with a labeled subsection naming the kind of partner/capability being sought (text label, not a color treatment).
6. **People, history, and IP** — founder background/origin story, team, company history, patent detail, stated as credentials. An active, granted-and-reissued patent in three countries is unusual and should read as the accomplishment it is, not as a caveat. Patent lives here only, kept conceptually separate from §4 evidence — but "kept separate" means don't present a patent as proof of performance, not "apologize for having a patent."
7. **Future applications** — brief, clearly conditional ("if validated..."). No dollar figures, no revenue projections, no specific business terms.
8. **Get involved** — three CTAs, one per audience path from the sitemap above.

## Voice: confident, not defensive

This section governs every page, not just the homepage. Read it before writing or editing copy.

**The failure mode to avoid is not "too much confidence" — it's disclaimer stacking.** Earlier drafts of this site over-corrected against genuine early hype (see "Old superlative-era language" below) by hedging almost every sentence, repeating the same "this isn't proven yet" disclaimer three or four times across a single page, and leading with the gap in evidence before establishing what's actually real. That reads as a team unsure of its own work, which is worse for credibility than the overclaiming it was meant to fix — and it undersells genuine, real achievements: a founder who has spent nearly a decade building and refining this modeling himself, across dozens of scenarios; an independent PhD engineering cross-check landing within ~2%; a patent that is not just filed but granted and reissued in three countries. Those are facts, not opinions, and they should be stated as the accomplishments they are.

**The line that must never be crossed is different from tone: never state an unmeasured outcome as if it were measured.** Don't say "TWT eliminates your cooling bill." Do say "TWT is modeled to eliminate cooling on all but ~10 days a year in [climate], based on [named source/method]." That is not a hedge — it's a citation, and a specific, sourced citation is *more* persuasive than a vague one, not less. Labeling a claim honestly is not the same as apologizing for it.

Working principles:

- **State the evidence label once, near the claim, and move on.** If a page's evidence status is already established once (in an evidence ladder, an evidence-page link, or a footer disclaimer), don't repeat "this hasn't been field-measured" again in the next three sections. One clear, well-placed label is honest. Three is defensive.
- **Lead with the fact, not the caveat.** Write "TWT operates at 65–75°F, independently cross-checked to within ~2% of the founder's own modeling" before "this has not yet been field-measured in a real building" — not the reverse. The achievement is the sentence; the evidence status is the qualifier, not the headline.
- **State real achievements as achievements.** A granted, reissued, multi-country patent; a decade of self-driven engineering work; a named, credentialed independent reviewer whose numbers matched within 2% — these are specific, factual, and defensible. State them plainly and once. Don't bury them inside a sentence whose actual job is to hedge something else.
- **The founder's own expertise reads as serious and professional, not formally academic — say so plainly either way, don't blur it.** Sandefur is not a PhD. Don't imply otherwise, and don't need to — what he actually did carries its own weight without borrowing credentials: nearly a decade of self-driven engineering work, building and refining thermal models by hand across dozens of scenarios, handling substantial datasets and simulations himself before any outside party reviewed it. That is a real, professional-grade body of work in its own right. The independent PhD-level check from University of Southern Indiana *confirms* that work; it isn't what makes the work legitimate in the first place — don't write as if the founder's numbers only became real once a PhD looked at them. The overall impression a reader should come away with: this team has come a long way, has done the homework, and is serious about seeing this through — a practitioner's rigor, correctly labeled as such, not dressed up as more formally credentialed than it is, and not undersold either.
- **A modeled number with a named source is a legitimate claim, not a weak one.** "Modeled by [name/institution], using [dataset/method]" is a complete, citable claim. Treat it with the same declarative confidence you'd give a measured result — the label is what keeps it honest, not what should make it sound tentative.
- **Omitting a non-essential caveat is editing, not dishonesty.** Not every open question needs to be surfaced on every page. The Evidence page is where the full, honest accounting of what's modeled vs. measured lives — other pages can state what's real confidently and link there, rather than re-litigating the evidence status inline every time a number appears.
- **No superlative language, still** ("most transformative in history," etc.) — this rule is unchanged, but don't confuse it with the rule above. A superlative is an ungrounded, unspecific claim of greatness. A specific, sourced achievement statement ("independently cross-checked to within 2%," "active patent in three countries") is not a superlative — it's a fact. The goal is *specific and confident*, not *vague and grandiose* — those are opposite failure modes, and both are currently guarded against.

**Voice check before publishing a sentence:** does this state a real fact confidently, or does it exist only to remind the reader that something isn't proven yet — a reminder that already appears elsewhere on the page? If it's the second thing, cut it.

### Headings and structure should create pull, not just report

Content should make a reader want to keep scrolling, open the next collapsed panel, or click through to a deeper page — not because anything is withheld or teased dishonestly, but because it's written to surface something specific and worth knowing before it explains it in full. A curious reader digs deeper; a bored one stops at the first paragraph.

- **Write headings that name a specific, real hook — not a generic label.** "Core Operating Principle" describes a section; it doesn't make anyone want to read it. "Why does five degrees change everything?" or "The same trick that keeps Mammoth Cave at 54°F, indoors" points at something the reader will actually want the answer to. Every heading should pass this test: would a curious, busy reader stop scrolling because of this line?
- **Open with the interesting part, not the setup.** Lead a section with the specific fact, comparison, or finding that makes the mechanism worth understanding — the wood-stove-sliced-into-512-pieces example, the Mammoth Cave comparison, the finding that a two-sided ICF wall never delivers its stored heat to the room at all under the modeled conditions — then explain the physics underneath it. The hook earns the explanation; the explanation shouldn't have to earn the reader's attention on its own.
- **Curiosity is not the same as vagueness or clickbait.** A heading that teases without paying off, or a claim withheld to manufacture suspense, violates the no-superlative and no-hype rules just as much as an overclaimed number would. The hook must be a real, specific, honestly-answered fact — the curiosity should come from what's genuinely surprising about the physics and the work itself, never from withholding information the reader is owed.
- **Label expandable "engineering detail" panels by what's actually inside, specifically enough to be worth opening.** "Engineering detail" invites nobody to click. "The exact model inputs Dr. Field used — element count, time steps, and material properties" tells a technical reader there's something concrete waiting, and gives a general reader clear permission to skip it without feeling like they missed something essential.

### Old superlative-era language (why the hedging exists, and why it overcorrected)

Older marketing material and AI-drafted analyses in the source archive contain language like "most transformative technology in human history," comparisons to penicillin, Nobel Prize speculation, and lives-saved projections built on stacked, unvalidated assumptions. That language is exactly what the "no superlatives" and "label every number" rules exist to prevent, and those rules stay. But the correction went further than intended and started treating *every* confident, sourced statement as suspect too. The target is the midpoint: specific, sourced, and stated once — not vague and grandiose, and not hedged into invisibility either.

## Content rules (apply sitewide, not just homepage)

- Every performance number must be labeled modeled or measured, with its source named. State it once, clearly, near the number — don't restate the label every time the number recurs on the same page.
- No superlative or ungrounded language ("most transformative in history," etc.) anywhere — see the distinction from specific, sourced achievement statements above.
- Patents and performance evidence are always kept conceptually distinct — a patent is proof of originality, not of performance. State this once per page where relevant, as a clarifying fact, not as a recurring apology.
- Do not name any of the following anywhere on the site right now: Honeywell, SafeEvac, Clemson University. These are live but unconfirmed conversations. Describe the type of partner being sought instead.
- Do not name most pre-2018 partners/supporters (old log-home manufacturer, concrete-form supplier, green-building nonprofit) — those relationships are stale. The one exception: the independent university modeling verification can be referenced (status of that specific relationship today is uncertain — describe the work done, don't assert it's ongoing).
- Do not discuss what a funder/research partner receives in exchange for money (equity, royalties, sponsorship, etc.) — this is intentionally undecided and off-limits for now. Funding CTA stays general ("let's talk"), not a specific offer.
- No revenue figures, licensing fee amounts, or market-projection numbers from internal planning documents anywhere public-facing.

## Design and tone direction

Visual register: serious engineering/research organization — not a startup, not a green-building lifestyle brand. Precise diagrams, visible sourcing/methodology, restrained typography, real technical drawings over stock photography. Confident and plain — see **Voice: confident, not defensive** above for the writing-tone equivalent of this rule; visual restraint and confident, unhedged prose are the same instinct applied to two different mediums.

Avoid: floating gradient blobs, glassmorphism, pill-shaped buttons, smart-home stock photography, leaf/eco iconography, vague sustainability language, animated statistics without a source.

## Open items (don't invent answers — flag if content depends on these)

- Whether the university partnership (independent modeling verification) is still an active relationship today.
- Final claims table (canonical modeled figures, signed off, since source documents contain conflicting versions of the same numbers — don't pull raw percentages from old material without checking for this). As of the most recent founder review pass, several of these are resolved (operating range 65–75°F, collector efficiency 69% modeled from Asheville, NC data) — check the latest founder-reviewed draft in hand before defaulting to older source documents, several of which contain superseded numbers or an uncorrected arithmetic error in the wood-stove/surface-area example.
- Real patented-block specifications beyond the outside dimensions (8×8×16, founder-confirmed as shareable) — cavity width, crossmember geometry, and the patent drawings themselves are still needed before diagrams can be redrawn around the current block instead of the older one-sided ICF.
- Even where these remain open, state what *is* known about them confidently and specifically — an open item is a gap to be closed by a research partner, not a reason to hedge the parts of the page that don't depend on it.

## Curated source material — and why it must never be copied verbatim

`TWT_Essential_Documents/` in the repo root is a curated, integrity-verified subset of the full source archive (two Downloads folders, ~72 original documents), organized by evidence type with a manifest explaining what was kept, what was left out, and why. Start there — `00_START_HERE.md` — before going back to the original archive folders.

**Read it for facts. Never write from it for prose.** It is written in a deliberately conservative, research/legal register — correct for its job, which is telling you precisely what is and isn't verified. It is not a style guide, and it should never be lightly paraphrased into site copy — if a page's wording starts to resemble it, that page has drifted back into the bland, defensive register this document exists to move away from. Pull the fact, the number, the source, and the evidence boundary from it — then write the actual sentence fresh, in the confident, benefit-forward voice defined in **Voice: confident, not defensive** above.

Concrete example of the transformation every piece of site copy should go through:

- **Source register** (`TWT_Essential_Documents`, correct for its own purpose): "Founder calculations modeled a projected efficiency gain; this has not been independently measured in a field setting and should not be presented as demonstrated."
- **Site voice** (correct for the website): "The founder modeled a 39–60% heat-pump efficiency gain from operating at 65–75°F instead of 105–120°F — independently cross-checked by University of Southern Indiana engineering faculty to within 2%. A built demonstration is the next step."

Same fact, same evidence label, same honesty boundary — opposite feel. The first reads like a disclaimer memo written to protect against a claims audit. The second reads like a team that knows exactly what it has, is proud of it, and is already moving toward proving more. The site should always read like the second one. This applies to every page, not just the homepage — including any page drafted by pulling numbers, mechanisms, or evidence status out of `TWT_Essential_Documents` or the wider archive.

One more instinct worth naming directly, since "confident" and "careful" can get conflated: **write toward the reader's "why this matters to me" before circling back to how it's proven.** A homeowner, a corporate partner, and a university don't open the page wanting a claims audit — they want to know what this could do and why it's worth their attention, stated plainly, with the proof close behind it rather than in front of it. Selling the idea and being factually careful are not in tension. Lead with the benefit and the achievement; follow with the method and the source. Never the reverse.
